01 · The problem

Every monsoon, the same roads go under — and the ambulance route is still chosen as if they didn't.

  • Who decides: the ambulance dispatcher, and the city flood-control team.
  • The decision: which route and which hospital on a heavy-rain day — and which roads to fix first.
  • The gap: navigation apps react to traffic and reports after a road is blocked. A dispatcher planning a trip needs to know before.

Our question

Can public terrain + road data tell us which roads are likely to flood, and what that does to an ambulance trip?

Mumbai floods in the same places year after year. BMC itself lists hundreds of chronic waterlogging spots. Yet the route an ambulance takes is chosen by tools that only react once a road is already blocked. Our user is the dispatcher and the flood-control team; the decision is the route, the hospital, and what to fix first. One number we computed ourselves: 96% of heavy-rain days fall in June to September — so this is a predictable, seasonal problem worth planning for.

HONEST BY DESIGN

A planning tool, not a flood detector

It ranks roads by terrain. It does not know water is on a road right now, and says so on screen.

The product is three steps. Pick a rain day — a real historical day or tomorrow's forecast — and the most flood-prone roads light up. Set the patient by clicking the map. FloodRoute finds the nearest of 35 major hospitals and draws two routes along real streets: the normal one and a flood-safe one that avoids the marked roads. You see the extra minutes, a different hospital, or "cut off". Every marked road explains itself, and there is a page that shows how reliable the score is. We are deliberate that this is a planning tool, not a live flood detector.

Why one library: the map, the route and the evaluation all use the same flagging rule, so the app can never disagree with the test.

05 · Method — and why we chose it

A transparent score, not a black box — because we have 14 labels, not 14,000.

Flood-proneness score per road (0–1)

SignalWhy it mattersWeight
Lower than the area around it (300 m)Water collects in local bowls0.45
Low absolute heightTide-locked drains back up0.25
Close to sea / creek / canalOverflow and tidal backflow0.20
Underpass / subwayThe lowest point on the road0.10

Each signal is rank-normalised. Weights were fixed before looking at any validation result. Bridges and flyovers are never flagged.

From a rain day to blocked roads

IMD category (mm/day)Roads treated as likely to flood
Moderate (15.6–64.4)top 3%
Heavy (64.5–115.5)top 8%
Very heavy (115.6–204.4)top 15%
Extremely heavy (≥ 204.5)top 25%

This mapping is an assumption — nobody publishes it. It is shown in the app, the user can change it ("What if?"), and we vary it in the sensitivity test.

Routing: fastest path to the nearest hospital on the full road graph, then again with the flagged roads removed. No path → "cut off".

Why a rule-based score and not machine learning? Because we have about fourteen reliable labels, not fourteen thousand. A model trained on fourteen points would just memorise them. So we built a transparent score from four terrain signals with weights fixed before we looked at any result. Then we need to turn a rain day into blocked roads. Nobody publishes "at 150 millimetres these roads flood", so this is an explicit assumption — shown in the app, adjustable by the user, and varied in our sensitivity test. Routing is then simple and checkable: fastest path normally, and again with the flagged roads removed.
06 · What the data told us

Three findings that changed how we read our own tool.

FINDING 1

Heavy rain is a monsoon problem

110 days of ≥ 64.5 mm in 21 years; 96% fall in June–September, peaking in July.

FINDING 2

Weather grids miss the extremes

On 26 July 2005 the reanalysis shows 69.6 mm. So we use rain data only to pick and label real heavy days — never as a water depth.

FINDING 3

Reported spots are inland bowls

Three findings. One: heavy rain is a monsoon problem — 110 heavy days in 21 years, 96% between June and September, peaking in July. Two: the weather grid misses extremes. On 26 July 2005 the gauge reported around 944 millimetres; the reanalysis grid shows 70. So we only use rain to pick and label real heavy days, never as a water depth. Three — and this one surprised us: the reported flood spots are not closer to the sea and not lower overall. They are local bowls inland. That told us Mumbai's waterlogging is a drainage problem more than a coastline problem.

Computed from data/processed/edges_scored.parquet and validation_spots_evaluated.csv: AUC = mean percentile rank of spot roads among 9,746 segments; 2,000 bootstrap resamples (seed 42). Elevation-only baseline ranks roads by low absolute height. Weights were not changed after this result.
This is the slide we are proudest of, because it is honest. We tested the score against 13 reported flood spots. Overall it is no better than random: AUC 0.46, with a wide interval because the sample is small — and an elevation-only baseline does no better. But look at where it fails. It catches the true sunken bowls — Churchgate, the Sion flood gauges, Oval Maidan. It misses Milan Subway because the 30-metre surface model sees the rail embankment, not the underpass beneath it, and Gandhi Market reads as higher ground, most likely because of flyovers and buildings in the surface model. So the failure has a cause we can name — and a fix: bare-earth elevation and drainage data. We did not re-tune the weights to make this number look better.

09 · Limits — and what would make it a predictor

What could make our conclusion wrong.

What it does not know

  • It is not a flood forecast: it ranks roads by terrain, not water on the road right now.
  • No drains, pumps or tide — the main drivers of Mumbai waterlogging are not open data.
  • The rain → roads share is an assumption, adjustable and tested, not measured.
  • 30 m surface model includes buildings and flyovers (the Gandhi Market miss).
  • 13 reported spots, biased to busy roads → wide uncertainty.
  • 35 destination hospitals are our curated list of major hospitals (BMC municipal + large general hospitals; Breach Candy and GT are not mapped in OSM); only main roads are modelled, so "cut off" means no safe main-road route.

Evidence that would raise confidence

  • Bare-earth elevation (LiDAR / DTM) instead of a surface model.
  • BMC drain and pump data and tide gauge levels.
  • Dated, event-level waterlogging reports or flood-gauge time series — this is what turns a ranking into a real prediction.
  • A curated list of emergency-capable hospitals from BMC / 108 EMS.
  • Ambulance trip logs from monsoon days to measure real delays.

Responsible use: a prototype for planning, not an official warning system or a substitute for expert judgement.

What could make us wrong. It is not a flood forecast. It does not know about drains, pumps or tides — which drive most Mumbai waterlogging — because that data is not public. The rain-to-roads share is an assumption. The elevation model includes buildings and flyovers. And thirteen spots is a small test. Each of these has a concrete fix: bare-earth elevation, BMC drain and tide data, and dated waterlogging reports — the last one is what would turn this ranking into a genuine prediction.
